Everything on the bench, in one place

Each part of the app reads from the others. A project knows your parts, the idea board knows your tools, the printer page knows its own limits, and the shopping list knows which shop really delivers fastest.

How I actually use it

Real questions from my own bench, and where the answer comes from.

"What can I build tonight?"

The idea board sorts my parts into roles (controller, sensor, display, actuator) and only suggests builds it can name every part for. Then it says how to box it, based on my tools: print it, buy a project box, or find a case made for the board.

Pair a Pi with an analog sensor and it adds an ADC to the list, ticked if I own an MCP3008. An ESP32 doesn't get that line.

ideas · parts · workshop

"Will Nylon CF print on my Ender 3?"

Each printer is recorded properly: bed, nozzle and its material, hotend and bed limits, enclosure, direct or bowden, filament drying. The answer comes back "not as it stands" with the five reasons and what each one costs to fix.

An unrecorded figure counts as unknown, not zero, so a half-filled form doesn't produce a printer that can't heat.

"Reprint that bracket."

Sliced G-code is kept against the part it was for. Next month's reprint is one button, sent straight to the printer over Klipper or OctoPrint. The dashboard shows what every printer is doing without waiting on one that's switched off.

For new parts, the case finder searches Printables for enclosures made for that board and measures the STL so I know it fits before a six-hour print.

printers · cases · parts

"Which ESP32 is in the greenhouse?"

The shelf counts six ESP32s. The firmware page records which one is in the greenhouse running what, keeps the image with its SHA-256, and gives the exact command to put it back: esptool with the right chip, avrdude for an Uno, or BOOTSEL-and-copy for a Pico.

"Is the homelab up, and what's it drawing?"

The homelab is a shelf that's switched on. Each machine gets its address, OS, the install image and its published checksum, and its power draw. The page checks each one over TCP (no root needed) and adds up the watts, leaving out machines with no figure instead of counting them as zero.

"Stop buying the same BME280 three times."

Every drawer gets a QR label. Scan it with a phone and the part's page opens with the count buttons under your thumb. Paste a product link from Adafruit, Pimoroni or The Pi Hut and it fills in name, photo, price and part number. Prices are tracked per shop, and orders learn each shop's real delivery time.

Who it's for

Anywhere a group shares a shelf of parts and a few machines, and nobody remembers which drawer the last one went in.

School makerspaces

Named accounts with an activity log, so a teacher can see who took the last servo. QR labels on every bin.

Robotics teams

Projects reserve their parts, so the build team sees what's actually free. BOMs import from KiCad and EasyEDA.

Library and community labs

One printer page that tells a visitor what each machine can print before they try.

Repair cafés

Firmware images and flash commands kept with each board, so a fix can be repeated by someone else.

Small hardware startups

Price history per shop and real delivery times for planning a build. Backups are one file.

Home labs

Machines, their images and checksums, and the power bill, next to the parts that built them.

Small on purpose

One Go binary, one SQLite file, one folder of photos. No database server, no npm install, no config file. Upgrading is replacing the binary; the database migrates itself.

Things I wrote rather than imported: a KiCad footprint parser and renderer, an STL parser with a z-buffer rasteriser for previews, and a paste-a-list parser so you never fill a form forty times.

An AI model is optional: Ollama, any OpenAI-compatible endpoint, or Anthropic. Whatever it suggests is checked against the real inventory; claim a part I don't own and it goes on the shopping list.
